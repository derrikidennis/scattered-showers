This dashboard shows the weather at three weather stations: Fort Wayne, Elgin and Kingsburg. Each one reports every few minutes. Use it for current conditions, the forecast, radar, storm alerts, and how much rain or snow has fallen.

On a phone or iPad, where this guide says click, tap.

The top of the page

One thing before the tasks. The row of controls near the top stays on screen while you scroll. It sets what the charts and tables show. It does nothing to the forecast or the radar.

  1. Station list. One place, or all three.
  2. Time buttons. Today, Last 24 hours, Last 7 days, Last 30 days, Everything saved.
  3. Pick dates. Choose your own start and end.

Leave it alone and you get today. That's usually what you wanted.

See the weather right now at a place

Open the dashboard. The first thing on the screen is three cards, one for each place. That's the answer. The rest of this guide is for when it isn't enough.

  1. The place. The dot next to the name is explained below.
  2. The temperature at the station right now, in degrees Fahrenheit.
  3. How much the temperature changed in the last 3 hours (▲ warmer, ▼ colder), and when the station last reported.
  4. Humidity, wind, air pressure, rain or snow so far today, today's high and low, and what it feels like outside. Wind shows speed and direction. A gust is a short burst faster than the steady wind.

Elgin and Kingsburg work the same way. On a phone, tap a picture to open it full size.

Each card has its own color along the top edge. That color follows the place onto the charts, so you can tell the lines apart.

Elgin shows Central time and Kingsburg shows Pacific. Each place uses its own clock. That's on purpose, and it will look wrong until you remember it.

What the dot means

  1. Green dot. The station is reporting.
  2. Full-strength number. It's current.
  1. Yellow triangle. No reading for more than 30 minutes. The station is quiet.
  2. The number fades. Read it as old.
  3. The last reading time tells you how old.

A yellow triangle means the station stopped talking. It doesn't mean the weather stopped. Could be wifi, could be power, could be a squirrel. On a computer, point at the triangle and it tells you how long it's been quiet. Task 11 has the longer history.

Look closely at one place

The cards are the summary. Each place also has its own page.

Two ways to get there

  1. Click anywhere on the place's card. The whole card works, not just the name.
  1. Or use the Station list at the top of the page. Click it and pick a place.

The list has four choices: All stations (overview), Lafayette Fields (Fort Wayne, IN), House of Shih Tzus (Elgin, TX) and Boys' Town (Kingsburg, CA).

What you see on a place's page

  1. Temperature.
  2. Feels like. The temperature once wind or humidity is counted.
  3. Humidity.
  4. Wind. Speed, direction, and the compass.
  5. Pressure. Air pressure, in inches.
  6. Precipitation. Rain or snow so far today.
  7. Today's high and low.

Click any tile to open a chart of it. That's task 9.

Everything further down

  1. Right now. The tiles above.
  2. Hourly. The next 48 hours (task 4).
  3. Forecast. The next 5 days (task 4).
  4. Radar (task 5).
  5. A chart of the tile you picked. Temperature to start with (task 9).
  6. Daily summary. One row per day (task 10).

See what's coming: the next few hours and days

Two things on a place's page look ahead. Hourly covers the next 48 hours. Forecast covers the next 5 days.

The next few hours

  1. The hour. The first card says Now.
  2. The expected temperature.
  3. Wind, in miles per hour, and the direction it blows from.

A blue percentage under the temperature is the chance of rain. It only shows up when the chance is 10% or more. No number means rain is unlikely.

The strip scrolls sideways. Drag it, or swipe on a phone. A bold day name (Fri) marks where midnight falls.

The next 5 days

  1. The day. In the evening the first card says Tonight.
  2. High, then low. Tonight's card has a dash where the high goes, because the daytime is over.
  3. Chance of rain and wind. Sunrise and sunset come last.
  4. An extra sentence, only when there's something extra: when the rain comes, fog, gusts, or how much rain to expect. A card with nothing extra has no sentence.

The chance of rain is the higher of the day and the night. In the picture, Monday says Partly Sunny and still shows 51%, because the rain comes after dark. When that happens, the icon turns to rain, the words say what's coming (Partly Sunny, then Chance Showers And Thunderstorms), and the extra sentence starts with "That night".

The hourly strip and the daily cards both come from the National Weather Service, so they tell the same story.

The time buttons at the top don't change either one.

Watch the radar

The radar shows where rain and snow are right now, plus the last hour.

  1. Scroll down a place's page to the Radar section.
  2. It plays on its own. 12 pictures, 5 minutes apart, then a pause on the newest one, then it starts over.
  1. Pause holds on one picture. The button then says Play. Press it to start again.
  2. The slider picks a particular picture. Dragging it also pauses.
  3. Alert outlines shows or hides storm warnings and watches on the map. The text next to it counts them, here "2 nearby: 1 warning, 1 watch". Task 6 explains them.
  4. Full screen makes the map bigger. Clicking the map does the same. To come back, press Close, or the Esc key.

The purple dot is the weather station.

If the radar can't load, the page says "No radar right now" and names the two services it tried. That's their problem, not yours. Try again in a few minutes.

Find out if there's a storm warning

If the National Weather Service has issued a watch or a warning that covers a place, the dashboard says so in three spots.

A watch means conditions could produce the weather. A warning means it's happening or about to. Warnings are the serious ones.

Where to look

On the overview, the place's card gets an orange tag that says how many alerts apply, for example "1 alert for this location".

On the place's page, a colored bar sits above the tiles. It names the alert and says when it ends. The color follows the type of alert.

Click the bar to open it.

The opened bar has four parts. What it says is the weather service's own text. What to do is their advice. Where lists the counties. Issued by names the office. The text is theirs, written in their style, often in capitals.

On the radar, the same alerts are drawn as outlines. A solid outline is a warning. A dashed one is a watch. The outline is thicker if the place's address is inside it. In full screen, click an outline to read its popup. It says if your address is inside, and when it ends. With none nearby, the radar says "No watches or warnings nearby."

How fresh is it

The page checks for alerts every 5 minutes and shows "Alerts checked" with the time, at the top right. If that's more than 15 minutes old, the note turns gold and says how many minutes ago:

See how much rain or snow fell

The Precipitation tile on a place's page shows rain and melted snow so far today, in inches of water.

During rain it adds a rate line, such as "rate 0.07 in/hr".

How snow is counted

Rain and snow are measured differently. Rain is counted in inches of water. Snow is counted in inches of snow, and snow is fluffy. About 10 inches of snow melts down to 1 inch of water. So the dashboard turns snow into water and adds it to the rain gauge's reading, which keeps one number that means the same thing for rain, snow, or both.

The big number is the total in water. Under it, the rain gauge's reading and the snow depth. In the picture: rain 0.08 in, snow 2.4 in. Snow divided by 10 is 0.24 in of water. Add the rain's 0.08 and you get the 0.32 in at the top. The glass fills white for the snow part and blue for the rain part.

The cards on the overview and the Daily summary use the same rule. They write it as the total with the snow in brackets, for example 0.32 in (snow 2.4 in).

Ten to one is an average. Wet, heavy snow packs down to less snow per inch of water, and light powder gives more. This is a rule of thumb, so don't be surprised when your ruler disagrees.

The snow number is an estimate from a weather model (Open-Meteo), not a measurement from the station. The page only shows it when the estimate is 0.05 in of snow or more, and it hides it if the estimate is more than 6 hours old.

For more than today, click the Precipitation tile. That opens a chart of rain and snow per day (task 9).

Look at a past day or week

The buttons at the top set how far back the charts and tables go.

  1. Pick dates opens a small box.
  1. Click a time button: Today, Last 24 hours, Last 7 days, Last 30 days, or Everything saved.
  2. Or click Pick dates, choose a From date and a To date, and the charts follow.
  3. To go back, click Back to today.

Everything saved goes back to the day collecting started. That's as far back as the history goes. The page doesn't have weather from before that.

Forecast and radar ignore these buttons. They only show now and ahead.

See how something changed

Every tile on a place's page opens a chart of that thing, for the time range chosen at the top. Not all the charts look alike.

  1. Click a tile.
  2. Scroll down to the chart. The heading changes to match what you picked.
  3. On a line chart, point at the line to read the value at that moment. On a phone, touch it. The arrow keys also work on a computer.
  4. Click Table, at the top right of the chart, for the same numbers as rows. The button then says Chart. Click it to go back.

A line breaks where the station stopped reporting. That's a gap in the data, not a wiggle in the weather. Times are the place's own clock.

Which tile gives which chart

Temperature and Feels like open the same chart, "Temperature and feels like". It has two lines: temperature, and a dashed gray one for feels like. When you look at it, the two lines often sit on top of each other and look like one line. That means the air feels like the thermometer says. Where they pull apart, wind or humidity is changing how it feels.

Humidity is one line, in percent.

Wind opens "Wind speed and gusts". Two lines again: the average wind in the place's color, and a dashed gray line for gusts. Gusts are always at or above the average, so the dashed line sits on top.

Pressure is one line, in inches. It moves slowly. Don't expect drama.

Today's high and low is different. It shows one point per day, not a line through the day. A solid line for the highs and a dashed line for the lows, with a dot for each day. Pick Today and you get a single dot of each. Pick Last 30 days and you get a month of them.

Precipitation is different too. It's bars, not a line: one bar per day, for rain from the gauge. When snow has fallen, a second bar appears for the snow estimate. It's drawn in white, with an outline, and it reads on a separate scale on the right side of the chart. Point at a bar and the box shows the rain gauge reading, the snow estimate, and the water total. Days with nothing have no bar. Table here has a column for rain and, if there was snow, one for snow.

With snow in the mix it looks like this. The purple bars are rain from the gauge, read on the left. The white bars are the snow estimate, read on the right.

See a day's high and low, and the records

The Daily summary is one row per day, for the range chosen at the top.

Each row has the high and low, what they felt like, rain or melted snow, the strongest wind and gust, and the pressure range. Today's row says "so far", because the day isn't finished.

Click a row to open the day. The arrow turns from ▶ to ▼. Click again to close it. Pick Today and the one row is already open.

An opened day shows a table of highs, lows and averages for temperature, feels like, dew point, humidity and rain, and for wind and pressure. Under that are sunrise, sunset and the hours of daylight, and a line with the record high and low for that date.

Highs and lows are whole degrees, because that's what the station reports. Records come from NOAA's long-running station at Fort Wayne International (history from 1939), not from the backyard station, so they're the nearest record, not the exact one. If your station tops the record, the line says so.

Check that a station is working

At the bottom of each place's page is Reporting health. It answers one question: is the station talking?

The heading gives the short version, like "clear for 25 h", meaning no gaps in the last 25 hours. The first table lists the latest outages: when the station went quiet, when it came back, and how many minutes. An outage is any silence over 10 minutes, since a reading should arrive every 5.

The second table is per day. Readings is how many arrived. Coverage is that as a percentage of a full day, and Silent min is the total quiet time. A normal day has 288 readings. The two clock-change days have 276 and 300.

A few gaps of 10 to 20 minutes a week is normal for a station on home wifi. Many in a row, or hours of silence, mean something at the station needs a look: power, wifi, or the unit itself. That's Derrik's problem, so tell him.

The yellow triangle on the cards (task 2) is the same idea, shown right where you'd see it.

Get the latest numbers

The page updates itself. It checks for new numbers every minute and reloads when there are some.

The weather stations report to the internet every few minutes. The dashboard takes a live reading for the Right now cards every 5 minutes, saves the full history every 15 minutes, and checks alerts every 5. So the Right now numbers can be a few minutes behind the station, and the charts a little more. That's the normal delay.

  1. Refresh data. Asks for the newest numbers right now.

The "Updated" time next to it says when the page was last built. Check it first if a number looks stale.

Something looks off

Look for the cause in this order.

  1. Is there a yellow triangle on the place's card? The station stopped reporting. The number is old. Nothing to fix on your end.
  2. Check the "Updated" time at the top. If it's old, the page is behind, not the weather.
  3. Check the time buttons. Charts and tables show whatever range is picked. A quiet chart may just be Last 30 days when you wanted Today.
  4. Check the clock. Elgin is on Central time, Kingsburg on Pacific.
  5. Reload the page.

Still wrong? Tell Derrik which place, what you saw, and what time it was. A screenshot saves a lot of guessing.

If the radar says it can't load, or the alert note says it couldn't reach the weather service, the weather services are down. The dashboard is fine.

Appendix: words used here, and where the numbers come from

Words used here

Station. A weather instrument set up at a house. This dashboard has three.

Feels like. The temperature once wind or humidity is counted.

Gust. A short burst of wind faster than the steady wind.

Watch. Conditions could produce dangerous weather.

Warning. Dangerous weather is happening or about to.

Quiet. A station that hasn't reported for more than 30 minutes.

Outage. A silence of more than 10 minutes between readings.

Radar. A map that shows where rain and snow are falling.

Where the numbers come from

Current conditions are from three Acurite personal weather stations, collected through Weather Underground (the Right now readings every 5 minutes, the saved history every 15). The 5-day forecast and the hourly strip are the National Weather Service's. Alerts are the National Weather Service, checked every 5 minutes. Radar comes from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet, with RainViewer as a backup. Records are NOAA's. The snow estimate is Open-Meteo's weather model.
